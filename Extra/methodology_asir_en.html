    METHODOLOGY¶

    Vocational module Proyecto Intermodular (Cross-Module Project) of the higher-level VET cycle in Network Systems Administration — ASIR. IES Macià Abela — Crevillent (Valencian Community, Spain). Teacher: Francisco Javier Hernández Illán.

    1. General approach and active methodologies¶

    For the Cross-Module Project I have chosen an approach based on Project-Based Learning (PBL) and Challenge-Based Learning (CBL), fully aligned with the «integrative and challenge-driven» nature that current Valencian regulations (Ordre 8/2025 and the Resolution of 17/07/2025) give to this module, within the framework of Organic Law 3/2022 and Royal Decree 659/2023.

    Core idea of the module

    The classroom runs as a sustained professional simulation. Students do not study isolated technologies: they operate a complete IT infrastructure that grows throughout the school year, just as a systems department would in a small or medium-sized company.

    The whole year revolves around a single company project, inspired by real organisations from the Crevillent area (UNIFAM, CreviPlay, local car repair shops, etc.). On top of that, I combine the following active methodologies on a daily basis.

    1.1. Active methodologies I combine¶

    • Project-Based Learning (PBL). The entire year revolves around a single simulated-company project. Every activity, guided practice and challenge becomes part of that project, which the team keeps until the final defence.
    • Challenge-Based Learning (CBL). Each work unit takes the shape of a numbered group challenge (RG301, RG401, RG501/RG502, RG601/RG602, RG701/RG702, RG801) with its own rubric, deliverables and defence. Students always know what they must produce and how it will be assessed.
    • Collaborative learning. Students work in cooperative teams of 2 to 3 people, with explicit role assignment (systems, networking, databases, security, documentation). Blockers, conflicts and rework are part of the learning process — they are not hidden.
    • Industry-grade approach. The tools we use are not didactic substitutes: they are the ones students will find in their first job — Git, GitHub, Docker, Docker Compose, Terraform, Ansible, AWS (VPC, EC2, RDS, S3, CloudWatch, IAM), Bind9, Nginx, WordPress, MySQL, Prometheus, Pandora FMS, MkDocs Material. This forces them to read official documentation in English, interpret real logs and learn to research on their own.
    • Incident handling as content. Failures are not hidden — they are part of the assessment. Every incident (a misconfigured Security Group, a non-persistent Docker volume, a non-idempotent ansible-playbook, a DNS server that does not resolve) goes into the project's incident log with its root cause and applied fix.
    • Continuous technical documentation. The project report is not a final annex but a living deliverable from day one, written in Markdown and published with MkDocs Material on GitHub Pages. Students soon internalise the module's rule: «if it is not in the report, it does not exist».

    • Progressive infrastructure deployment. Each work unit adds a new layer to the system:

      network → compute → storage → application → integration →
security → automation → operations

      Students do not «build a project»: they operate an infrastructure that keeps growing.

    1.2. Connection with the technical axes of the ASIR cycle¶

    These methodologies tie together the main contents of the ASIR cycle:

    Technical axis Technologies students work with
    Linux Ubuntu Server 22.04, Debian 12
    Networking and services DHCP, DNS (Bind9), SSH, FTP, Apache, Nginx
    Virtualisation GNS3, virtual machines on LliureX
    Containers Docker, Docker Compose, networks and volumes
    Automation Ansible, Terraform
    Service integration Multi-service Compose stack or several interconnected EC2 instances
    Systems administration Users, firewalling, hardening, backups
    DevOps documentation Versioned IaC, idempotent playbooks, reproducible runbooks

    What separates a student from a professional

    Students learn to operate a system that is not theirs and must remain understandable to someone else. That change of perspective — writing and configuring with the next operator in mind — is the real leap between having taken the module and being ready to work.

    2. Methodological guidelines and learning challenges¶

    The module is organised into eight work units (UT) that progress through the school year with increasing complexity. They are not independent blocks: each unit adds a layer on top of the previous one, on the same project.

    2.1. The eight work units¶

    UT Title Main LO Output added to the project
    1 Project toolset LO3 · LO4 Working environment: Git, GitHub, MkDocs, Docker, GNS3, AWS
    2 Challenge framing and context analysis LO1 · LO2 · LO5 Company characterisation, SWOT, scope, requirements
    3 Solution design and planning LO2 · LO5 Architecture, data model, WBS, schedule, risks
    4 Iterative development and testing LO3 · LO4 Deployed stack (Docker track or AWS track)
    5 Integration, security and validation LO3 · LO4 Integrated stack, hardening, monitoring
    6 Deployment and operations LO3 · LO4 Infrastructure as Code (Terraform) + Ansible
    7 Technical documentation and final report LO4 · LO5 Report published on GitHub Pages
    8 Public presentation, defence and closure LO5 Public defence with a live demo

    Each work unit ends with a group challenge worth 30 points and its own rubric. This gives the year a clear cadence, identical to that of a professional project cycle:

    framing → design → development → integration → deployment → documentation → defence

    2.2. Two parallel tracks: Docker and AWS¶

    From UT3 onwards, each team picks a technical track. Both tracks share the same learning outcomes and the same integrated rubric for the final defence, but they let students specialise in a specific professional profile.

    Aspect Docker track (RG501 / RG601) AWS track (RG502 / RG602)
    Infrastructure Docker Compose on a Linux host VPC, several EC2 instances, Elastic IP
    Components Nginx, WordPress, Laravel, MySQL EC2 web, EC2 app, EC2 MySQL, EC2 DNS (Bind9)
    Security Nginx hardening, protected /admin, tested backups Security Groups, IAM/MFA, hardened SSH
    Observability Prometheus + Pandora FMS + Compose logs CloudWatch + instance logs
    Automation (UT6) Ansible on the host Terraform + Ansible
    Professional profile Linux / container sysadmin Cloud administrator

    2.3. Guidelines I apply consistently¶

    • Incremental work. Students never start a challenge from scratch: every UT builds on the previous deliverable. What is deployed in UT4 is integrated and hardened in UT5, automated in UT6 and documented in UT7.
    • Partial deliveries with a rubric. There is no single final exam. Every UT closes with a deliverable and a public rubric published on Aules and openly applied in class. In some cases there is also an intermediate defence.
    • Continuous integration. We version everything on GitHub: IaC code, playbooks, docker-compose.yml files, configurations and documentation. Once the team is mature, changes go through Pull Requests peer-reviewed by teammates.

    • Technical validation with verifiable criteria. Every challenge defines objective criteria:

      • the website responds at a given URL,
      • the database is not reachable from outside its network,
      • a second ansible-playbook run returns changed=0 (idempotency),
      • terraform destroy && terraform apply reproduces the full environment,
      • backups are successfully restored.

    • Functional and negative tests. Students must prove that what should work, works — and that what should not work, does not work either: MySQL denied from unauthorised sources, /admin properly protected, SSH attempts without a valid key blocked, and so on. Negative tests are explicit evidence in the UT5 rubric.

    • Permanent documentation. From day one, every team keeps an MkDocs site with the project's report. I review it weekly. If a technical decision is not documented, it does not count as done.

    • Defence of the work performed. The year culminates in a public defence of 30 to 35 minutes before a panel:

      Stage Duration
      Presentation 10-12'
      Live functional demo 8-10'
      Technical defence + Q&A 8-10'
      Self- and peer-assessment 3-5'

      Any team member must be able to answer about any part of the project: this is the real filter for shared authorship.

    • Iterative improvement. After every delivery I give detailed feedback with the rubric in hand. Subsequent versions incorporate that feedback, exactly as happens with code reviews in a real company.

    Outcome at the end of the year

    Every team ends the course with:

    • a genuinely operable project,
    • a Git repository with a complete history,
    • a public report on GitHub Pages,
    • a reproducible infrastructure that any team member could rebuild from scratch in a few minutes.

    3. Technology ecosystem and working tools¶

    The choice of tools defines the work culture of the module. I have deliberately chosen an ecosystem that is open, industry-standard and centred on versionable plain text, where documentation, source code and infrastructure are all managed with the same techniques.

    Four tools are central and hold up the rest of the stack:

    flowchart LR
  A[**Aules**<br/>Moodle - GVA] --> P[(Team<br/>project)]
  G[**GitHub**<br/>+ GitHub Pages] --> P
  M[**Markdown**] --> P
  K[**MkDocs Material**] --> P
  P --> Eval[Final defence<br/>+ published report]

    3.1. Aules — the Valencian Government's Moodle platform¶

    Aules is the official platform of the Valencian Department of Education, built on top of Moodle. It is the pedagogical backbone of the module.

    What it does in the course:

    • Lesson planning: structure of the eight work units, milestone calendar and links to the resources published on GitHub Pages.
    • Student tracking: attendance, grading of every challenge, learning-outcome-level follow-up and cumulative record of evidence.
    • Communication: notices, announcements, internal messaging and forums attached to each UT.
    • Task submission with rubrics imported in CSV (current official format). Students submit links to their repository, MkDocs site, demo videos and self-assessments.
    • Module organisation: a single, self-contained view of the whole course, accessible from any device and free of proprietary dependencies.

    Aules gives me full traceability of assessment and provides students with a single official reference for deadlines, rubrics and grades.

    3.2. GitHub — central repository for every project¶

    GitHub hosts both the teacher's module guide (pi_asir) and each student team's repository.

    What we use it for:

    • Version control. Every change to source code, configuration (docker-compose.yml, *.tf, Ansible YAML) and documentation is logged with author, date and commit message.
    • Collaborative work. Branches, Pull Requests, peer reviews and merges into main. This is the natural workflow of any modern IT team.
    • Professional workflows. README.md, LICENSE, .gitignore, Issues for incident tracking, Releases with tags (v1.0-final) and, optionally, GitHub Actions workflows to publish the report automatically.
    • Versioned documentation. The report lives under /docs in the repository, written in Markdown and versioned exactly like source code. It becomes an auditable artefact.
    • Work traceability. Commit history and Pull Requests make it possible to demonstrate who did what and when. This is decisive at the final defence when assessing shared authorship.

    Every team ends the year with a public URL to its report thanks to GitHub Pages, accessible to the assessment panel, the families and, eventually, potential employers.

    3.3. Markdown — single authoring format¶

    We use Markdown for everything: notes, challenges, rubrics, project reports, user manuals and administrator manuals.

    Why Markdown:

    • Standard for technical documentation. It is the de facto format for repository README files, wikis, documentation platforms such as MkDocs / Hugo / Docusaurus, and the wider DevOps ecosystem.
    • Simplicity. Focus is on content and structure, not on layout. This lowers the barrier of entry during the first weeks.
    • Reproducible and versionable. Plain text, diffable in Git, free of proprietary formats and compatible with any editor (VS Code, Cursor, vim, Aules plugins). It works the same on Linux, Windows and macOS.
    • DevOps standard. The Infrastructure as Code culture assumes that everything is versionable text. Docker READMEs, Ansible guides, Terraform documentation and AWS templates are all written in Markdown.

    Underlying idea

    In this module, writing documentation is writing code. The quality of technical writing is a professional skill on its own.

    3.4. MkDocs Material — professional web publishing¶

    MkDocs Material turns the project's Markdown files into a professional website. For me it is not a cosmetic choice: it forces the documentation to have a real, public, verifiable output.

    What students get from MkDocs Material:

    • A published site with search, sidebar navigation, light/dark mode, admonitions, Mermaid diagrams and syntax highlighting, without touching HTML or CSS.
    • Living documentation: every push to main automatically deploys the report to GitHub Pages. It never stays «on someone's laptop».
    • Structured organisation: mkdocs.yml forces students to think of their report as a hierarchical site (landing page, chapters, annexes, glossary) and to separate diagrams, screenshots and source code into folders.
    • Recognisable technical look and feel: the result resembles the official documentation of Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform or FastAPI. Seeing their own work presented in that format changes how students perceive it.
    • Open-source culture: the tools we use are open-source projects; configurations can be shared and improvements can be contributed back to the community.

    3.5. What the ecosystem delivers as a whole¶

    The combination Aules + GitHub + Markdown + MkDocs Material gets students — almost without noticing — to acquire real professional habits:

    • they produce technical documentation continuously,
    • they maintain versioned projects,
    • they publish technical content automatically,
    • they work with the same flows as any IT/DevOps team.

    And it connects directly with key concepts of the industry:

    • Infrastructure as documentation. What you define in *.tf and *.yml files is not only deployment code — it is also the most reliable documentation of your system.
    • Reproducible documentation. A report written next to the code and published automatically is the only kind that does not become outdated.
    • Modern workflows. Branches, Pull Requests, reviews, releases, continuous deployment.
    • Collaborative culture. Asynchronous work, written technical discussion, traceable authorship.
    • Automation. The module ends with students who can move from manual deployments to fully automated, code-driven ones executed with a single command.

    4. Student groupings¶

    Classroom organisation varies depending on the activity, but a stable pattern runs through the whole year.

    4.1. Working modes¶

    • Individual work for initial exploration activities (Docker installation, first Git commands, first MkDocs site, early AWS Educate or GNS3 labs) and for the self-assessment stage of each UT. The aim is to consolidate the basics individually before cooperating.
    • Pair work for specific guided practices (pair-troubleshooting on a broken Compose stack or a non-idempotent playbook) and for cross-team Pull Request reviews. This is the ideal format for two people to think out loud about the same technical problem.

    • Cooperative teams of 2 to 3 students acting as stable project units throughout the year. At the start of the course each team picks:

      • one technical track (Docker or AWS),
      • one local business case (UNIFAM, CreviPlay, a car repair shop, a local association…),

      and sticks to that decision until the final defence.

    • Whole-class sessions for unveiling new challenges, intermediate defences, UT retrospectives, joint incident analysis and discussion of architectural decisions.

    4.2. Role distribution within the cooperative team¶

    Roles are inspired by real systems-administration teams:

    Role Areas of responsibility
    Sysadmin / Linux Users, permissions, firewall, backups
    Network / Cloud Docker networks or VPC, Security Groups, DNS
    Database MySQL/PostgreSQL, data structure, backup and restore
    AppDev / Web WordPress/Laravel, web stack, integrations
    DevOps / Docs Terraform, Ansible, MkDocs, Pull Requests, releases

    Roles rotate partially to avoid rigid specialisation.

    Rule for the final defence

    Every team member must understand the whole stack. At the defence, the panel is allowed to question any member about any part of the project.

    Technical decisions are documented inside the repository (in the project's Markdown, in Issues or in Pull Request comments), so students get used to a shared and traceable decision-making flow, equivalent to that of any professional IT team.

    5. Types of activities¶

    I run a varied set of activities throughout the year, all of them tied to the team project. None of them is an isolated exercise: each activity produces evidence that goes into the repository and the report.

    5.1. Technical activities¶

    • Service deployment. Apache, Nginx, MySQL/PostgreSQL, WordPress, Laravel, Bind9, DHCP, FTP and SSH, in Docker containers or on EC2 instances.
    • System integration. End-to-end connection between web, application and database; Docker networks (frontend/backend); isolation with Security Groups; internal DNS and name resolution between components.
    • Linux administration. Users, permissions, systemd services, firewalling (UFW or firewalld), scheduled tasks, log management, SSH hardening and OS hardening on Ubuntu Server 22.04 or Debian 12.
    • Network configuration. Topologies in GNS3, AWS VPCs with public and private subnets, Internet Gateways, Route Tables, Security Groups, Elastic IPs and firewall rules inside containers.
    • Automation.
      • Ansible: playbooks and roles (common, docker_host, nginx_proxy, backups, monitoring, web, app, db, dns, hardening), static and dynamic inventories, Jinja2 templates, variables, secret encryption with ansible-vault, and idempotency verification with --check --diff.
      • Terraform (AWS track): manifests (main.tf, variables.tf, outputs.tf) and the init → plan → apply → destroy cycle.
    • Incident handling. Diagnosing real problems: containers that cannot resolve names, lost volumes, MySQL connections blocked by a Security Group, mis-issued HTTPS certificates, non-idempotent playbooks. Every incident is recorded in the incident log of the project report.
    • Infrastructure maintenance. Updates, periodic backup verification, restoring snapshots into a clean environment, change management through git revert + a new apply, and recurring idempotency checks.

    5.2. Validation and documentation activities¶

    • Functional and negative tests.

      Type Examples
      Functional «the site responds at http://…», «persistence survives docker compose down && up»
      Negative «port 3306 is not reachable from unauthorised sources», «/admin requires authentication», «a second apply introduces no changes»

    • Technical writing. A living report in Markdown, published with MkDocs Material on GitHub Pages. Structure: characterisation, design and planning, development, integration and security, deployment and operations, testing and evidence, conclusions and annexes (user and administrator manuals, glossary, bibliography).

    • Autonomous research. Reading official documentation in English (Docker, AWS, Terraform, Ansible, Bind9, Nginx, Material for MkDocs), critical source evaluation and responsible use of AI — with explicit declaration of usage, sources and licences, in line with the module's assessment criteria.

    5.3. Presentation and deep-dive activities¶

    • Oral presentation and technical defence. Intermediate defences per UT and a public final defence before a panel (presentation 10-12', live demo 8-10', Q&A 8-10', self-assessment 3-5'). All members must speak and any member must be able to answer about any part of the project.
    • Feature extension. Deep-dive activities for faster-paced students: HTTPS with Certificate Manager, CloudWatch alarms, Prometheus + Pandora FMS, load balancers, incremental backups, cross-team Pull Request reviews. These are credited as bonus points in the rubric.

    5.4. Cross-cutting activity: AWS GameDay¶

    I integrate participation in the AWS GameDay 2025 — the Spanish national AWS challenge — as a realistic production-pressure simulation: students solve incidents on cloud architectures under time constraints. It is one of the activities that best consolidates the AWS track and an excellent showcase opportunity for the school.

    6. Closing remarks¶

    The methodology of this module does not aim to teach technologies one by one. It aims to train professionals capable of operating a complete infrastructure, of documenting what they do and of defending it before a technical interlocutor.

    Every methodological decision is oriented towards that goal:

    • the challenge-driven design,
    • the dual Docker / AWS track,
    • the intensive use of GitHub, Markdown and MkDocs Material,
    • the Aules-driven workflow,
    • and the integrative final defence.

    This is the very same methodology I am happy to share with my colleagues at our Erasmus partner school, as the foundation of an ongoing networked collaboration between European vocational IT programmes.
